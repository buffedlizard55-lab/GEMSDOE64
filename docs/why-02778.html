Why 0.2778 was the family's best score

ORGANIZER-CONFIRMED receipts inverted through the verified closed form of the official metric

The question

Why did h33-h33-2-b2-20261004T220000Z-e5eb6e7e-zeros score 0.2778 — the highest live score in this family of repositories — and can it be beaten?

Short answer

Because it is the only artifact in the registry that removed the one thing the metric punishes and the catalogue cannot reward: probability mass within 200 m of an already-mapped fault. Its parent scored 0.2708 with 40,199 dots; the child scored 0.2778 with 37,654. The 2,545 dots that were deleted realised about 0.0161 DTI-credit each, against 0.1022 for the dots that were kept — a 6.4× depletion. Deleting them was not a stylistic choice; it was required by the metric's own marginal rule, whose bar at DTI 0.2708 is 0.0542. A dot earning 0.016 against a bar of 0.054 is a strictly negative trade, because DTI charges α = 0.2 for every unit of emitted mass and refunds almost nothing for it.

The mechanism, in full

Write the metric in closed form. With T = TP_w, S = Σp, M = Σ_x p(x)·kmax(x), K = |G|, α = 0.2, β = 0.8:

DTI = T / ( α·(T + S − M) + β·K + ε )

This identity was re-derived independently in this repository and verified against the component form to 1.1e-16 (Experiment 1, check C3), and FN_w = K − T was verified to hold exactly, difference 0.0 (check C2).

Now invert it. Given a live score and a known emission mass S, the realised credit is

T = DTI · ( 0.2·S + 0.8·K )

K is not directly observable, so the inversion is run as a profile over the plausible range of K rather than as a point estimate. For a single raster, c and K are not separable — but for a controlled pair the difference is robust, because the same K appears in both and largely cancels. The registry happens to contain exactly one such pair.

An independent cross-check

A second, unrelated estimate comes from a different pair in the same family. Going from d2.8 (44,090 dots, 0.2600) to h33-2-b2-like flank removal adds ΔS = 6,436 dots for ΔT = 88, i.e. 0.0137 credit per dot — within 15% of the 0.0161 from the controlled pair. Note also that d2.8 has exactly 6,436 dots within 2 px of the catalogue, the same number as the mass difference: the flank band is the mass difference between those family members.

Why the depletion exists physically

Two effects compound, and they point the same way:

  1. Masking. DrivenData staff confirmed (forum thread 11516, 16 Sep 2026) that pixels corresponding to known USGS/INGENIOUS faults are excluded from evaluation and from re-evaluation. A 300 m kernel centred on a dot near a mapped fault has a large fraction of its support inside the masked set, so there are fewer scorable truth cells for it to earn credit from. Mass on catalogue pixels earns no TP and incurs no FP — it is pure waste.
  2. Label construction. The Prize Rules (§2) state the label universe includes faults "newly identified by geology experts at NLR and USGS". A fault that an expert has newly identified is, by construction, one that was not already mapped. The hidden truth is therefore depleted near the published catalogue for a second, independent reason.

Can 0.2778 be beaten?

Yes, but not by better emission. The arithmetic is explicit. At the child's mass S = 37,654 and K = 7,905, reaching the top of the verified leaderboard snapshot (0.3262, 2026-10-04) requires

T_needed = 0.3262 · (0.2·37,654 + 0.8·7,905) = 4,518
c_needed = 4,518 / 37,654 = 0.1200     (against 0.1022 today, i.e. +17.4%)

Meanwhile the family's own mass series says emission is already at its optimum and past it: going from 37,654 dots to 44,090 adds 6,436 dots for 88 credit, i.e. 0.0137 each — far below the 0.0556 bar. Adding dots cannot help. Nor can removing more: the b2 artifact already has zero dots within 2 px.

So the remaining +17.4% is a field problem, not an emission problem. It has to come from putting the same number of dots on ground that is more often really faulted — which means a detector that finds faults the catalogue does not contain. That is exactly what the candidate hypotheses page is about.

The negative control that matters

sgmc-off-catalogue-44k emitted 44,090 dots taken from the USGS State Geologic Map Compilation traces lying beyond 300 m from the published catalogue — the single most tempting "free known-real faults missing from the catalogue" idea available. It scored 0.0512. Its median distance to catalogue was 18.0 px, comfortably off-flank, and only 647 dots landed on the catalogue itself.

Being off-catalogue is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. A source being a real mapped geological feature says almost nothing about whether it is in the hidden label set. Any hypothesis of the form "emit known lineaments that the catalogue lacks" is refuted by this receipt, and should not be retried without a mechanism that predicts which of them the NLR/USGS experts would have newly identified.
