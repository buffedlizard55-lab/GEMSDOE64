One hypothesis of this general shape was tested live and failed decisively: emit the USGS SGMC fault traces lying beyond 300 m of the published catalogue — 21,160 features, 82,151 footprint pixels, of which 61,664 (75.1%) are more than 300 m from the given catalogue. That is by far the largest pool of known-real faults absent from the catalogue available for free.

It scored 0.0512 — ORGANIZER-CONFIRMED against a family best of 0.2778. See the negative control. The lesson constrains every row above: a feature being real geology is not evidence it is in the hidden label set. #1 — Clay-cap breach: high near-surface conductivity with a sharp lateral gradient

Expected DTI gain: medium-high  ·  Implementation cost: low

Layers: band 17 (conductivity at 1 km depth), band 15 (depth to basement), derived cond_grad, derived depth_shallow

Physical signature and transform: Alteration minerals (illite/smectite) seal a geothermal reservoir. A fault that breaches the cap expresses as a *lateral discontinuity* in conductivity, not a high or low value: the seal is offset across the trace. Transform = magnitude of the gradient of a 5-px-smoothed conductivity field, intersected with shallow basement, then thinned to ridge lines.

Why it catches a fault MISSING from the USGS/INGENIOUS catalogue: The catalogue is compiled from surface geologic mapping. A breached clay cap under alluvial cover produces no surface map unit boundary at all — it is visible only in the subsurface electrical model. That is the definition of a fault an expert would newly identify from geothermal data rather than from a map.

How it differs from anything already in the repo: Every existing conductor-based artifact in the sibling repositories used conductivity as a *scalar* predictor (high = prospective). None used its *gradient* as a lineament detector, and none intersected it with depth to basement. The repo's own factorial put the thermal/geochemical family at only +0.0044, which is consistent with a scalar treatment carrying little information — it does not test the gradient hypothesis.

External data needed: none — all inputs are in the hash-pinned training_features.tif.

Cost note: All inputs are already in training_features.tif. Implementable in one session; cond_grad is already built and in the canary table.

#2 — Relay ramps and step-over terminations between mapped trace tips

Expected DTI gain: high  ·  Implementation cost: medium

Layers: catalogue trace geometry (as *geometry only*, not as a proximity feature), derived dem_localrelief_r2, band 4 (geodetic second invariant)

Physical signature and transform: Where two mapped fault segments overlap in strike but are laterally offset, the connecting relay ramp is a zone of distributed deformation with a distinct topographic expression: an oblique ridge between two tip lines. Transform = tip-to-tip vector field, score candidate bridges by alignment with the local relief fabric and by geodetic second-invariant magnitude.

Why it catches a fault MISSING from the USGS/INGENIOUS catalogue: Faulds et al. report ~32% of Great Basin systems are step-overs/relay ramps and ~39% of geothermal systems are 'blind'. A relay ramp connecting two *mapped* segments is itself frequently unmapped, because neither tip looks like a fault trace on a map — the connecting structure is oblique to both.

How it differs from anything already in the repo: The repo family used the catalogue only for proximity and for buffer pruning. This uses it as a *graph* whose missing edges are the prediction target. That is a different question: not 'where is near a fault' (which Experiment 2 showed is credit-depleted) but 'where must a fault be, given two faults that do not connect'.

External data needed: none — all inputs are in the hash-pinned training_features.tif.

Cost note: Needs a trace-skeletonisation and tip-detection step, then a bridge search. No new data. This is the highest-gain candidate but it is the one most at risk of collapsing back into catalogue proximity — the leakage canary must be run on it explicitly, and it should be rejected if its single-feature blocked AUC exceeds 0.90.

#3 — Drainage deflection and beheaded channels on the 100 m detrended DEM

Expected DTI gain: medium  ·  Implementation cost: medium

Layers: band 12 (detrended elevation), band 19 (detrended elevation slope), derived dem_tpi_s5, derived dem_fabric_aniso

Physical signature and transform: A fault scarp deflects or beheads streams. Transform = flow-direction divergence from the regional trend, plus abrupt changes in channel longitudinal gradient, both measured on the detrended DEM so the regional tilt is removed. Score = density of deflection vertices per unit length of a candidate lineament.

Why it catches a fault MISSING from the USGS/INGENIOUS catalogue: Drainage anomalies are exactly the evidence geomorphologists use to map faults *not* expressible in bedrock geology — i.e. faults hidden under basin fill. They are routinely absent from a compilation built on map-unit contacts.

How it differs from anything already in the repo: The repo's DEM work used curvature, scarp response and local relief — all *amplitude* measures. Drainage deflection is a *vector-field* measure (direction change), which is orthogonal to amplitude and is not captured by any existing layer. The canary shows dem_fabric_aniso is the weakest of the DEM layers at 0.5944, so directional information is currently under-exploited.

External data needed: Better: the USGS 3DEP 1 m DEM, from which the sibling repositories already derived lidar_scarp_features_u8.tif. Free and official: usgs.gov/3d-elevation-program. Obtainability already verified by a prior bridge (706/716 tiles); the derived raster is recoverable from GEMSDOE24@07345ea0 rather than re-downloaded.

Cost note: Implementable on the supplied 100 m DEM alone, but at 100 m most channel deflection vertices are unresolved; the 1 m DEM materially changes the expected gain. Test on 100 m first as a cheap falsification.

#4 — Magnetic-gradient lineament fabric, not magnetic amplitude

Expected DTI gain: low-medium  ·  Implementation cost: low

Layers: band 3 (TMI horizontal gradient), band 9 (TMI vertical gradient), derived tmi_grad_ratio, derived tmi_gradmag

Physical signature and transform: Fault zones alter magnetic mineralogy, producing a *ratio* anomaly between the horizontal and vertical gradient rather than an amplitude anomaly. Transform = ridge extraction on tmi_grad_ratio, restricted to cells where the ratio's local variance exceeds its regional baseline.

Why it catches a fault MISSING from the USGS/INGENIOUS catalogue: Magnetic expression of a fault is an alteration signature, not a lithologic contact, so it is systematically absent from a surface-geology compilation.

How it differs from anything already in the repo: The repo's own factorial scored the potential-field family at −0.0027 — i.e. *negative*. But that tested amplitude. tmi_grad_ratio reaches 0.677 single-feature blocked AUC in this repository's canary, which is higher than the official tilt band (0.634), so the ratio transform carries signal the amplitude treatment does not.

External data needed: none — all inputs are in the hash-pinned training_features.tif.

Cost note: tmi_grad_ratio is already built and canary-tested. Cheapest candidate on this list; can be falsified in under an hour.

#5 — Quaternary volcanism as a fault-permeability indicator

Expected DTI gain: low  ·  Implementation cost: low

Layers: gdr_volcanic_vents_in_footprint.csv (672 bytes — very few vents), great_basin_q_volcanics (6,776 px), band 17 (conductivity)

Physical signature and transform: Magma and hydrothermal fluids use faults as conduits. Quaternary vents and volcanics align along fault-controlled permeability. Transform = distance to the nearest Quaternary volcanic contact, used as a *prior* on fault presence rather than as a direct predictor.

Why it catches a fault MISSING from the USGS/INGENIOUS catalogue: Faulds et al. note hydrothermal outflow can surface kilometres from its source, so the vent field marks the *plumbing*, not the fault — and the plumbing is not in any fault catalogue.

How it differs from anything already in the repo: No existing artifact used volcanics. However the vent CSV inside the footprint is only 672 bytes, so the in-domain sample is tiny; the Q-volcanics raster (6,776 px) is the usable layer.

External data needed: none — all inputs are in the hash-pinned training_features.tif.

Cost note: Ranked last: the repo's factorial put the thermal/geochemical family at +0.0044, and with 6,776 px of volcanics against 5.1 M scored cells the coverage is too thin to move a DTI of 0.28 materially. Include only as a prior, never as a primary emitter.
